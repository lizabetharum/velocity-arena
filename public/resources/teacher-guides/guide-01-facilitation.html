The Silence Protocol

Non-negotiable.

  • Ask the question. Stop talking completely.
  • Wait. Don't rephrase. Don't add "think about it." Don't nod encouragingly.
  • At ~7 seconds, scan the room for a raised hand or visible processing (writing, looking at data).
  • If no one responds at 8 seconds, use Turn and Talk — not a repeat of the question.
  • After Turn and Talk, return to the original question. You will get responses.
Why 7 seconds matters: research consistently shows that wait time under 3 seconds produces short answers from high-confidence students only. 7 seconds activates processing across all confidence levels. The discomfort you feel during those 7 seconds is productive. Don't resolve it early.
Gap Sentence Anatomy

Every gap sentence requires all three parts. Missing any one part means it's incomplete. Do not accept incomplete gap sentences.

Part 1 — Predicted Value
"We predicted our bot would score 2 points..."
Must be a specific number. "We thought we'd win" is not a predicted value.
→
Part 2 — Actual Result
"...but the actual result was 0 points..."
Must come from the Match Log. Not memory — the written record.
→
Part 3 — Quantified Gap
"...so our gap was 2 points."
Must be a computed number. "A lot" or "a little" is not acceptable.

Complete Example:

"We predicted our Motor% of 50 would let us clear the obstacle zone in 3 seconds, but the actual time was 5.2 seconds, so our gap was 2.2 seconds."

Incomplete (do not accept):

"We thought we'd be fast but we were slow." — No numbers. Not a gap sentence.

3-Step Loss Recovery Protocol

This is a 3-step protocol. Follow the sequence. Use it on match days 7–10 and 13–15, and any time a team shows visible emotional distress after a loss.

Step 1
Acknowledge the Emotion
30 seconds — no longer
Name what happened. Give the team 30 seconds to feel it. Don't minimize, explain, or redirect yet. Silence is fine here. The emotion is real and needs a moment.
"That was a tough result. Take 30 seconds. You don't have to talk."
Step 2
Locate One Decision in Data
With Match Log open
Ask them to find one — and only one — decision in their Match Log that connects to the result. Not a list of everything wrong. One decision. This restores analytical control.
"Open your Match Log. Find one decision — just one — that shows up in this result."
Step 3
Restore Hypothesis-Maker Role
Before they leave the table
End with a forward-looking hypothesis, not a verdict. The team leaves as scientists who have a next test, not losers who need to do better. This reframe is the whole point.
"What would you test differently next time? Finish this: '"We're changing __________ because ___________."
"We're changing the time input in our endurance equation from 3 minutes to 2 because our bot actually ran out of power before the match ended." ' "

Do not add a 4th step.

The most common facilitator error is adding "and here's what you should change" after Step 3. That fourth move steals the hypothesis from the team. They must generate it. Your job ends at "What would you test differently?"

3 Hardest Teacher Moves in This Program
01
Hold the Gap
You ask a question, no one answers, and you feel responsible for the silence. The impulse to rescue is powerful. Every time you give in, you train students that waiting is optional.
What to do instead:Count to 7 out loud in your head. If still nothing, use Turn and Talk — never restate the question first. The answer is in the room. Your job is to wait for it.
02
Press Correct Answers
When a student gives a correct answer, the natural move is to confirm it and move on. That kills the analysis. Correct answers deserve the same pressure as wrong ones — students need to know why the answer is right.
What to do instead:"That's correct — now explain the formula step that produces that number." Apply Press for Evidence even when (especially when) the answer is right.
03
Stay Out of Allocation
A team comes to you with a bad allocation. You can see it's going to underperform. The urge to redirect them is overwhelming. But the loss that follows a student-owned bad decision is the most powerful learning event in the program.
What to do instead:"Walk me through your reasoning." Ask questions until you understand their logic. Then step away. The data will do the teaching. Your job is to be ready with the loss-recovery protocol.

Observation Protocol — Match Day Timeline
  • Pre
    Before match starts: Scan allocation cards

    Check that every team's stats sum to 20 (Season 1) or the correct Season 2 budget. Look for teams with a single stat at 15+ — likely to fail and likely to blame the bot, not the allocation.

    Listen for: "The bot just doesn't work." — This is pre-match. It means the student doesn't trust the math yet.
  • M1
    During Match 1: Watch the Analyst

    The Analyst should be logging in real time, not from memory after the match. If the Match Log is blank during the match, the Analyst is not filling their role. Redirect immediately.

    Listen for: "I'll remember it." — Intervene: "The log needs to be filled during the match, not after."
  • →
    Between matches: Target the gap sentence

    Every team should write a gap sentence before their next match starts. Walk to the team that lost. Ask for the gap sentence. If they can't produce one, use the Press for Evidence move.

    Listen for: "We just need to go faster." — Ask: "What does your Speed value predict for Motor%? Does that match what you saw?"
  • L
    After a loss: Run the 3-step protocol

    Go to the team that lost. Do not go to the winning team first — they don't need you yet. The 3-step protocol starts with 30 seconds of acknowledgment. Don't skip it even when time is short.

    Listen for: Silence, heads down, team fragmentation (one student walking away). These are loss-spiral signals. Intervene immediately.
  • End
    Post-match debrief: Pick one data point per team

    Do not try to review everything. Pick the single most mathematically significant number from the round and ask the whole room to connect it to a formula. One number, one formula, one connection.

    Ask: "What one number from today's matches would change your allocation for next time — and which formula tells you why?"
