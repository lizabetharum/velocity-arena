Facilitation Guide

Talk moves · silence protocol · gap sentences · guidance ladder · 16-Day Edition

Facilitation Philosophy

Facilitators should model, clarify, and teach when needed. The goal is adult support that leaves students responsible for thinking. Guidance is appropriate when students are frozen, directions are unclear, or vocabulary is blocking progress. The tools below describe when to step in, and how to do it in a way that builds student capacity rather than replacing it.

Minimum Success Floor Before Day 1

Every session clears this bar

✓
Students participate
✓
Students feel safe to be wrong
✓
Students explain some reasoning out loud
✓
Facilitators avoid solving every problem
✓
Students revise after mistakes
If all five are true at the end of a session, that session succeeded. This floor exists to protect the work from perfectionism — a session where students are thinking, talking, and revising is a good session, even if the facilitation wasn't flawless. Measure up from here, not down from ideal.
Guidance Ladder Before Day 1
Use the lowest rung that works. Start at 1. Move down only when the student is genuinely stuck — not just slow. The more of the work students do, the more transferable the learning.
  • 1
    Pause
    Stop talking. Count 7 seconds. Most students will start working if you give them time and don't fill the silence.
  • 2
    Ask a question
    "What does your data show?" / "What formula are you using?" A question returns the thinking to the student.
  • 3
    Point to data
    Gesture to the Match Log or dashboard without explaining it. "Look here." Let them read it.
  • 4
    Clarify directions
    Re-read the task prompt or worksheet instruction with them. Confusion about what to do is different from confusion about math — fix the former, don't solve the latter.
  • 5
    Model one example
    Work through one step — not the student's problem, a parallel one. Then step back and let them apply it.
  • 6
    Give the method
    Explain the procedure. "Here's how percent change works." Don't solve their specific numbers — give them the tool.
  • 7
    Give the answer
    Only when safety, time constraints, or access requires it — or when a vocabulary barrier is blocking all progress and can't be resolved any other way.
    Use sparingly. If you reach rung 7 frequently, that's a signal to revisit the task design or the student's readiness for that concept.
Not all help is rescue. Clarifying directions, modeling one example, and naming a pattern are not rescuing. Rescue means doing the thinking students can still do.

The Silence Protocol Use during session

A tool, not a rule.

  • Ask the question. Stop talking completely.
  • Wait. Don't rephrase. Don't add "think about it." Scanning the room is fine.
  • At ~7 seconds, look for a raised hand or visible processing. Call on them.
  • If no one responds, use Turn and Talk — not a repeat of the question.
  • After Turn and Talk, return to the original question.
7 seconds activates processing across confidence levels. Under 3 seconds produces short answers from high-confidence students only. If a student is in genuine distress rather than productive thinking, respond to the student — the pause is in service of thinking, not a rule to hold at all costs.
Gap Sentence Anatomy Use during session

Every gap sentence requires all three parts. Missing any one part means it's incomplete.

Part 1 — Predicted Value
"We predicted our bot would score 2 points..."
Must be a specific number. "We thought we'd win" is not a predicted value.
→
Part 2 — Actual Result
"...but the actual result was 0 points..."
Must come from the Match Log. Not memory.
→
Part 3 — Quantified Gap
"...so our gap was 2 points."
Must be a computed number. "A lot" is not acceptable.

Complete Example:

"We predicted our Motor% of 50 would let us clear the obstacle zone in 3 seconds, but the actual time was 5.2 seconds, so our gap was 2.2 seconds."

Incomplete — ask for the missing part:

"We thought we'd be fast but we were slow." → "What was your predicted time, and what was the actual?"

Participation Balance Protocol Use during session

When one or two students tend to speak for the group, use these moves before stepping in directly. Structure tends to work better than naming.

Observable Signal
One student answers before teammates finish reading or calculating
Assign roles (Reporter, Recorder, Analyst). Direct questions to the role: "Analyst, what does the data show?"
Observable Signal
Quieter members are recording but not reasoning aloud
Use Turn and Talk directed specifically: "Everyone explains to a different teammate — not the person you usually talk to." Debrief the quieter voices first.
Observable Signal
A student defers repeatedly: "I don't know, ask them"
Don't redirect to a different student. Use the Guidance Ladder — start with a pause, then a question they can answer from what's visible: "What do you see in Match Log row 2?"
Observable Signal
A student argues dismissively with teammates' ideas
Use Agree/Disagree with a data requirement: "You disagree — what number in the dashboard supports your position?"

3-Step Loss Recovery Protocol Use during session

Use on match days 7–10 and 13–15, and any time a team shows visible distress after a loss. Follow the sequence.

Step 1
Acknowledge the Emotion
~30 seconds
Name what happened. Give the team a moment. Don't redirect yet. Don't extend this into a longer support session.
"That was a tough result. Take a moment."
Step 2
Locate One Decision in Data
Match Log open
One — and only one — decision that connects to the result. This returns analytical control to the team.
"Open your Match Log. Find one decision — just one."
Step 3
Restore Hypothesis-Maker Role
Before they leave the table
End with a forward-looking hypothesis. The team leaves as people who have a next test to run.
"One change. What is it? 'We're changing _____ because _____.'"

The protocol ends at Step 3.

Adding advice after Step 3 — "here's what you should change" — takes the hypothesis away from the team. Stop at "One change. What is it?" and let them generate the answer.

Good Enough Debrief Use after session

The minimum that works

"Pick one number. Ask what formula produced it. Ask what it changes for next time."

That's enough. You don't need to review every moment of every match. One number with a formula and a next-step question closes the learning loop.

3 Hardest Moves in This Program Use for coaching
01
Hold the Gap
After asking a question with no response, the impulse to step in is real. Students who don't answer quickly may still be processing — silence doesn't mean the question failed.
What to do instead: Count to 7. If still nothing, use Turn and Talk — never restate the question first.
02
Press Correct Answers
When a student gives a correct answer, the natural move is to confirm and move on. Correct answers deserve the same pressure as incorrect ones.
What to do instead: "That's right — show me the formula step that produces that number."
03
Stay Out of Allocation
A team presents an allocation likely to underperform. The urge to redirect is real. But a student-owned decision that doesn't work is valuable — the data does the correcting.
What to do instead: "Show me your steps." Ask questions until you understand their logic, then step back.
6 Common Facilitator Errors Use for coaching
#The ErrorWhy It Tends to HappenThe Fix
01 Accepting "I think" without a number Participation feels like it's happening "Which formula gives you that answer?"
02 Breaking silence before 7 seconds Silence can feel like failure on the facilitator's part Count silently. Post "7 sec" on a sticky note. The pause is working even when it doesn't feel like it.
03 Steering a team's allocation before matches You can see the allocation is likely to underperform "What does your data suggest?" then step back. Clarifying the budget formula is fine. Steering away from a decision you disagree with is not.
04 Accepting a 2-part gap sentence Two-thirds complete can sound complete "You have the prediction and the result — what's the computed gap?"
05 Improving Commissioner proposals during prep A weak proposal can feel like the facilitator's problem to fix Clarifying format requirements is fine. Improving the argument is not — weak proposals go to the floor, and the class response is the learning.
06 Adding a 4th step to loss recovery You want to help the team move faster Stop at "One change. What is it?" The hypothesis belongs to the team.
When You Feel Overwhelmed Use during session

Facilitator Recovery Sequence

  • → Stop talking.
  • → Give the room a 3-minute task.
  • → Sit down.
  • → Pick one problem, not five.
  • → Ask for data before giving advice.
  • → Resume the schedule from the next clean transition.
Presentation Support Boundary Before Day 1

Applies to Commissioner's Meeting (Day 12) and Championship pitches (Days 15–16). The facilitator's job is format compliance, not content direction.

Appropriate Before the Meeting

Confirming that a proposal cites at least two data points and names one math concept type. Clarifying what those format requirements mean. Reminding teams of the rubric criteria.

Not Appropriate

Suggesting which data point makes a stronger argument. Rewriting a sentence in the proposal. Telling a team their argument won't work. Let the proposal go to the floor as written.

During the meeting: redirect accuracy challenges to the room. "Does this proposal's data check out against the dashboard? Someone verify." Don't correct proposals yourself — that's the Commissioners' job.

Facilitator Coaching Checklist Use for coaching

For observers. Use during or after a session to give specific, actionable feedback.

  • Did the facilitator wait after questions (7 seconds before stepping in)?
  • Did students explain their reasoning — not just give answers?
  • Did the facilitator avoid taking over student thinking?
  • Did quiet students speak at least once?
  • Did losses lead to revision — not just disappointment?
