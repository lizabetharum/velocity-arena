Distance Lab — Answer Key

There is no single set of right numbers. Every bot has a different speed, so every team's answers are different — and different is correct. This key gives you one fully worked bot on the left and, for each step, the rule that tells you whether any team's answer is right on the right. Grade against the rule, not the numbers.

Do not project this. Steps 4 and the "Stop and look" box are supposed to be the students' own discovery. Read the key yourself and go ask questions at the tables.
The worked bot: measured 31, 69, 107, 145 cm at 1, 2, 3, 4 seconds. Its true model is distance = 38 × time − 7. Use it to sanity-check the shape of what you are reading; do not expect any team to match these exact numbers.

1 Find your bot's speed

Worked bot

1-second distance = 31 cm.
First speed guess = 31 cm/sec.

Check any team

Speed guess = their 1-second distance. Same number in both blanks. That is all this step is.

Range check: a bot's cm/sec is roughly its Speed stat × 7 or 8. A Speed-5 bot lands near 35–40; a Speed-10 bot near 70–80.

2–3 Predict 2 s and 3 s, then test

Worked bot

2 s: predict 31×2 = 62, actual 69, gap 62−69 = −7.

3 s: predict 31×3 = 93, actual 107, gap 93−107 = −14.

Bot went further than predicted, both times.

Check any team

Prediction = their speed guess × time. Must be filled in before the actual — that is the whole point.

Both gaps should be negative (predicted < actual), and the 3-second gap should be bigger than the 2-second gap. If a team's gaps are near zero, their bot barely accelerates — fine, and rare.

Gap = predicted − actual, so "further than predicted" makes it negative. Do not correct a team that circled "further."

4 Plot the three runs

Worked bot

Three dots at (1, 31), (2, 69), (3, 107). They lie on a straight line.

a. Yes — a ruler touches all three.
b. Carried back to 0 seconds, the line hits below 0.

Check any team

a. The three dots should be close to a straight line. Say yes unless one dot is wildly off (a measuring slip — have them redo it).

b. The right answer is below 0. Accept "below" or "a bit under zero." A team that says "at 0" hasn't extended the line far enough — point back at the graph, do not tell them.

"Stop and look" and "Talk to another team" boxes have no marks. You are checking that they wrote something and noticed the gap is growing and the bot goes further than predicted. The point is the noticing, not a correct phrase.

5 Subtract the two runs

Worked bot

107 − 69 = 38 cm.

"What does it represent?" → how far the bot goes in one extra second — its real speed.

Improved speed estimate = 38 cm/sec, bigger than the first guess of 31.

Check any team

Answer = their 3-second distance − their 2-second distance.

It should come out bigger than their Step-1 guess, because the 1-second run was dragged down by start-up. If it comes out smaller, they subtracted the wrong way round.

This equals cm-per-second only because the runs are 1 second apart. A team that used 2 s and 4 s must divide their difference by 2.

6 Find the start-up cost

Worked bot

A · full speed: 38×2 = 76 cm.
B · measured: 69 cm.

Start-up cost = B − A = 69 − 76 = −7 cm.

Check any team

Must be B − A (measured − full speed), and must be negative. This is the most common error: a team that writes A − B gets a positive number and their whole equation ends up wrong.

Fast check: the start-up cost should be close to their 2-second gap from Step 2. For the worked bot both are −7. If those two numbers disagree, something upstream is off.

7 Write the equation

Worked bot

distance = 38 × time − 7

y = distance, x = time, m = 38, b = −7.

Check any team

First blank = their improved speed (Step 5). Second blank = their start-up cost (Step 6), with its minus sign.

Because the sheet prints + before the second blank, a correct equation reads + −7, i.e. minus 7. Accept either + (−7) or a rewritten − 7.

8–9 Test the new equation, then an untested time

Worked bot

4 s: predict 38×4 − 7 = 145, actual 145, gap 0.

5 s (never driven): predict 38×5 − 7 = 183 cm, then measured.

Check any team

Prediction = their m × time + their b. The gap should now be small — a few cm — and much smaller than the Step-3 gap. That shrink is the point of the whole lab.

A zero gap is luck, not the bar. 2–3 cm is a success. If the gap is still large and negative, they likely kept the old speed or dropped the minus sign — walk back through Steps 5–7.

The column that tells you if the data is good is the last one. For any team it should be roughly constant — the same number down the column (38 here). That constant is their bot's speed, and it should match the improved speed they found in Step 5. If the "change" numbers jump around (say 38, 25, 41), the bot is measuring inconsistently: batteries or start line, before any more math.

11 The closing questions

  • Gap sentence: first blank = their Trial-2 gap as a positive number; last blank = their final gap as a positive number, which should be smaller. "The change that helped most" = using the difference-based speed and subtracting the start-up cost. Accept any wording that names those.
  • Why won't another team's equation match? (11a) Because the bots have different speeds — different m. Strong answers also note different start-up costs. This is the Model Swap idea arriving early; there is no single right sentence.
  • Which differed more, speed or start-up cost? (11c–d) Usually speed varies more team-to-team, because it comes straight from the Speed stat, while start-up cost is similar across bots. But accept whatever their two real numbers show — the skill is comparing their models honestly, not landing on "speed."
