Distance Lab

For the day when the bots never touch the ball. You do not need a working match to do real mathematical modelling — you need a bot that moves, a tape measure, and a prediction. Everything the project asks for can be done right here.

Why this lab exists. Zero ball touches does not mean the students failed. It means the bots cannot yet reach the ball — which is a distance-and-direction problem, not a soccer problem. This lab attacks that problem directly and produces the prediction data the week needs at the same time. Fix the bots by doing the math on them.
The one thing that can never be zero. Goals can be zero. Ball touches can be zero. But any bot whose wheels turn at all travels some distance, and a tape measure will always give you a number. That number is all the mathematics needs.

Setup — ten minutes

  • One long strip of tape on the floor per team, as a start line. Bots always start with wheels behind it.
  • One tape measure or metre stick per team. This is the most important object in the room today.
  • Measure in centimetres. Not squares, not "about a foot." Centimetres, written down.
  • Each team needs their MakeCode project open, because they will change one number in it repeatedly.

The lab — run this loop for the whole block

  1. Trial 1 is calibration: drive for exactly 1 second. Set the drive to 1000 ms, run it, and measure how far the bot travelled in centimetres.Write it down. This number is the bot's speed in cm per second. Every team's will be different — that is the point.
  2. Now predict, before touching anything. "If our bot drives for 3 seconds, it will travel ______ cm."They get this by multiplying: cm per second × 3. Written down and signed before the bot moves.
  3. Run it for 3 seconds. Measure the actual distance in cm.
  4. Gap sentence, out loud and written: "We predicted ___ cm. We got ___ cm. The gap was ___ cm. Because ___."Almost every team will travel less than predicted. That is real — the bot loses time speeding up at the start. Let them discover it; do not tell them.
  5. Change exactly ONE number in their model or their code, and write down which one.Usually: subtract a start-up lag from the prediction, or adjust the cm-per-second figure.
  6. Predict a new distance for a new time (try 2 s, then 4 s, then 5 s), run, measure, gap sentence again.Aim for five trials per team. Five predicted-vs-actual pairs is plenty of data for Tuesday.

The mathematics they are actually doing

This is not a consolation activity. It is a cleaner version of the same modelling the matches were supposed to produce — and the relationship here is genuinely linear, so the equation behaves:

Their model
distance = speed × time − startup lag
which is slope-intercept form
y = m x + b
  • m (the slope) is the bot's speed in cm per second — a number with a real physical meaning they measured themselves.
  • b (the intercept) is the start-up lag, and it comes out negative — the bot always falls short because it spends the first fraction of a second accelerating. Students discovering a negative intercept and being able to say what it means physically is a genuinely strong outcome.
  • x is drive time in seconds; y is predicted distance in cm.
Why this is better math than "goals vs. speed." Goals are noisy, low-numbered, and depend on the opponent. Distance is a clean linear function of time that every team can measure to the centimetre, and the parameters are numbers they control in their own code. When they change unitMs and the line moves, they are seeing a parameter change a model. That is Goal 1 exactly.

What success looks like today

  • Every team has five rows filled in. That is the whole target.
  • The gap column gets smaller down the page. Not perfectly, not for everyone — but the trend should be visible and they should be able to say why.
  • Every team can state their bot's cm per second and that it differs from the team next to them.
  • Nobody is troubleshooting a soccer match. If a bot drives straight for 5 seconds and lands where predicted, it can now reach a ball — you have fixed the actual problem.

If the bots will not move at all

Then it is a technical fault, not a maths problem. Work down this list, fastest check first:

  • Do the LEDs show a number? If not, the program did not flash. Re-copy the file to the micro:bit.
  • Fresh batteries, and the Cutebot's own power switch ON. This is the single most common cause.
  • Is the Cutebot Pro extension in the project? Without it the drive blocks do nothing.
  • Does the bot respond to a bare 1-second drive in a brand-new empty project? If yes, the fault is in their code, not the hardware.
And if only one or two bots work: run the lab on shared bots, rotating teams through. Each team still writes their own prediction before their turn and measures their own trial, so every team keeps its own data and its own model. Sharing hardware does not mean sharing an equation. If no bot moves, teach Tuesday's method on the practice dataset and be honest with students that the numbers are stand-ins.
