Facilitator Non-Negotiables

These are the commitments that protect the program's learning architecture. When any of them slips, student thinking degrades faster than most facilitators expect.

1
7-second wait time after questions — this is the standard, not a rigid countdown to fill

Seven seconds activates thinking across all confidence levels. Under 3 seconds, only high-confidence students respond. The goal is the thinking it creates — if a student is genuinely lost rather than processing, respond to them.

2
Run the 3-step loss recovery script on every losing team, every match day

No team skips. No team gets a shortened version. Every losing team completes all three steps before their next match begins. This is the single highest-dropout-risk moment in the program.

3
No blanks in the data dashboard

Every match gets a prediction logged before it starts and an actual logged immediately after. A prediction entered after seeing the result is worthless as data. Circulate and verify during every post-match window.

4
Redirect correctness questions — don't answer them

"Did we do this right?" → "What would a correct answer look like? Is that what you see?" The match result is the teacher. Your job is to keep students in contact with their own data, not to validate in real time.

5
Commissioner's Meeting: format compliance only, not proposal improvement

Before the meeting, confirm proposals include two data points and name one math concept. During the meeting, redirect accuracy challenges to the room. Weak proposals go to the floor — the debate is the learning.

6
Sentence starters posted and visible all four weeks

Post the four categories before Day 1 and leave them up. Students who struggle to start a gap sentence need the scaffold visible, not just mentioned once at the beginning of the program.

7
ENDS ritual runs every day — all 10 minutes

ENDS is not a wind-down. It is the day's consolidation moment. Each type targets a different metacognitive goal. Cutting it short cuts the learning architecture.

8
Improvise within the principles — don't improvise the principles away

You will make judgment calls every day. Make them in the spirit of the framework: keep thinking in students' hands, use data as the authority, treat confusion as information. The tools are yours to use flexibly.

Three Highest-Risk Facilitation Moments
Risk 1 — Day 1 (and Day 15)
Math Shame Re-Triggering on the Diagnostic
The pre-task diagnostic is the moment most likely to re-trigger math shame before trust is established. Students who struggle with math see a question sheet and immediately associate it with prior failure. The identical risk recurs on Day 15 with the post-task diagnostic.
Mitigation

Frame the diagnostic before distributing it using the exact script in the Day 1 risk box. Use non-bot contexts (speed, distance, ordered pairs) so no student feels disadvantaged by not yet knowing the stat system. On Day 15, use the "comparing you to yourself" frame explicitly.

Risk 2 — Every Match Day (Days 7–10, 13–15)
First Real Loss Without a Structured Recovery
The first Season 1 loss is the single highest dropout-risk moment in the program. Facilitators who improvise emotional responses cut losing teams off from their data and from their next iteration. This risk repeats on every match day and peaks at the Championship.
Mitigation

Memorize the 3-step script before Day 1. The 3-step sequence is the structure. Work within it. Step 1: acknowledge the emotion (genuinely, briefly). Step 2: locate the decision point in the data. Step 3: restore agency with a hypothesis.

Risk 3 — Day 12 (Commissioner's Meeting)
Facilitator Intervening
The Commissioner's Meeting and final match's pre-match pitch are the two moments of maximum student agency. Facilitators who correct proposals mid-presentation or suggest data to use undermine the agency.
Mitigation

Your only intervention during the Commissioner's Meeting should be redirecting accuracy checks to the room: "Does this proposal use the data correctly? Can anyone verify the math?" Clarifying how the budget formula works is fine. Improving what proposals say is not.

6 Structural Elements

These are structural scaffolding, not optional decoration. They create the social stakes that keep reluctant learners engaged on Day 8 and Day 12 — the high-dropout-risk days in any four-week competitive program.

Week 1, Day 2 · End of session
Bot Naming
The program's first permanent artifact. Bot names and origin stories are posted on the wall for all four weeks. Exhibition visitors read origin stories on Day 16, creating a narrative through-line from first day to last.
Week 2, Day 7 · First match day
Leaderboard
Updated after every match day. The Best Prediction column rewards mathematical accuracy, not just winning. A last-place team can lead the Best Prediction column — preventing early disengagement from teams that fall behind on wins.
Week 2, Day 8
Rivalry Cards
Data-backed trash talk. Each card must cite a specific stat or data point. This creates a social incentive to read and verify the opponent's dashboard data and do the math accurately before making a public claim.
Week 3, Day 11 · Public announcement
Underdog Mechanic
The last-place team gets 2 extra points to allocate before Season 2. Prevents the most common failure mode in competition-as-motivation programs: early losers who stop engaging because winning feels impossible.
Week 4, Day 16 · Awards ceremony
Best Teacher Award
Exhibition visitors vote for the student who explained the math most clearly — not the team that won. The program's closing statement: what matters is making mathematical thinking clear to someone who'd never heard it before.
Week 3 and 4 · Open Labs
Skills Arena
A dedicated side field for focused programming challenges. Teams attempt short missions, log predictions and actuals, earn visible Skill Badges. A parallel track for teams momentarily disengaged from the main competition.
Pre-Day 1 Preparation Checklist

Ten specific things to know, make, or practice before Day 1. Each represents a predictable new-facilitator failure when absent.

#ItemWhy It Matters
1
Calculate all four stat formulas for every point value 1–8 in under 10 seconds each
Speed: ×5%. Power: P². Endurance decay: P(t) = 100 - (20-E) × 1.5 × t. Turning: 0.2 + (T × 0.02). You cannot spot an allocation error in real time if you cannot produce the answer faster than a student can.
2
Memorize the 3-step loss recovery script word-for-word and practice it aloud 5 times
Step 1: "That was a tough result. Take a moment." (~30 sec, stop). Step 2: Find the one decision in the data. Step 3: "What's the one change you'd make? Write it as a hypothesis." You must deliver this automatically under emotional pressure.
3
Setup and program a BBC micro:bit + Cutebot Pro bot from scratch and debug at least two MakeCode errors
You cannot facilitate Match-Build-Make without hands-on hardware experience. You must have experienced a motor power percentage mismatch between MakeCode and bot behavior and traced the error back to the calculation.
4
Set up all the BBC micro:bit + Cutebot Pro bot and train them to recognize the black line. Install MakeCode.
Make sure laptops and robots are set up prior to day 1.
5
Pre-fill one complete sample data dashboard with fake Season 1 data, including 2 mismatches >20%
You only spot a blank prediction entry or percent change error if you know what a correct dashboard looks like. The most common error: students log predictions after knowing the actual outcome.
6
Physically rehearse all three improv activities required for Days 1–2
Sound and Movement (circle pass), Mirror (pairs), Failure Bow. These require you to model them, not describe them. Students take their energy cue from your willingness to look foolish first.
7
Practice three redirection responses until each is a reflex
(1) "Did we do this right?" → "What would correct look like?" (2) "What is the equation?" → "What two data points do you have?" (3) "What does this mean?" → "What does this number tell you your bot did?" Redirecting under pressure requires an automatic response.
8
Score two sample Commissioner proposals against the rubric before Day 11
The Commissioner's Meeting requires redirecting accuracy checks to the room in real time. You can only do this confidently if you can identify whether a cited data point is accurate and whether the rule addresses the last-place team.
9
Set up the physical room: four stat-labeled corners, driving question visible from every seat
Room environment is curriculum. If the driving question is not visible from every seat, it does not function as a driving question. If the bot-naming wall has labeled slots, it signals that something meaningful will go there.
10
Explain, without notes, why each stat is introduced on its specific day
Day 1: Speed only (maximum simplicity). Day 2: + Endurance (rate of change). Day 3: + Turning (coordinate geometry). Day 4: + Power (ratio). Day 5: Full budget equation. Each day is scaffolded — all four on Day 1 would collapse the learning sequence.
Sentence Starters — Post All Four Weeks

Post before Day 1. Leave them up. Students who cannot start a gap sentence need the scaffold visible, not just mentioned once.

Making a Claim from Data
"My data shows _____ because _____."
"When I changed _____ from _____ to _____, the result went from _____ to _____."
"The pattern I notice is _____. I know this because _____."
Describing a Calculation
"The percent change from _____ to _____ is _____% because I calculated (_____ − _____) ÷ _____ × 100."
"I used the formula _____ and plugged in _____ for _____, which gave me _____."
"My answer is _____. I got this by first _____, then _____, then _____."
Comparing Two Things
"_____ and _____ are different because _____. The numbers that show this are _____ and _____."
"Both _____ and _____ have _____ in common, but they differ in _____."
"_____ is better for _____ because _____, but _____ is better for _____ because _____."
Explaining a Mismatch
"The formula predicted _____, but the actual result was _____. The gap is _____ because _____."
"I expected _____ because _____, but instead I got _____. One reason could be _____."
