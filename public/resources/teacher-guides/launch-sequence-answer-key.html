Launch Sequence — Answer Key

Every team's numbers are different — different ball position, different bot. This gives you one fully worked bot on the left and, for each step, the rule for checking any team on the right. The worked bot: the teacher placed the ball 60 cm across, 80 cm up — so c = 100 cm, 4 blocks, angle 37°.

Set the ball on a Pythagorean triple if you want clean numbers. 30–40 (→50), 60–80 (→100), 90–120 (→150), or 50–120 (→130) all give a whole-number diagonal. It removes calculator fuss and lets you see instantly whether a team's diagonal is right.

1–2 Measure, then the distance formula

Worked bot · 60 across, 80 up

diagonal = √(across² + up²)

= √(60² + 80²) = √(3600 + 6400) = √10000 = 100 cm.

Check any team

Square each side, add, square-root. Watch two slips: squaring wrong (60² is 3600, not 120), and adding before rooting — √(a²+b²) is not a + b.

If you gave a triple, the diagonal should be the whole number (e.g. 100). If it isn't, their arithmetic is off.

3 Distance → blocks

Worked bot · c = 100

blocks = 100 ÷ 27 = 3.7 → round to 4 blocks.

Code: runBlockCnt(4).

Check any team

Their diagonal ÷ 27, rounded. One block is 27 cm, distance-based, so it is the same for a fast or slow bot — no speed or start-up involved here.

4 The angle

Worked bot · 60 across, 80 up

angle = tan⁻¹(60 ÷ 80) = tan⁻¹(0.75) = 37°.

Turn 37°, drive 4 blocks, turn back 37°.

The one rule, three cases

angle = tan⁻¹(across ÷ up).

· across = 0 → 0° (straight shot).
· across = up → 45° (e.g. 81/81; no calculator needed).
· otherwise → the tan⁻¹ (e.g. 60/120 → 27°).

Protractor on a scale drawing is a fine substitute for tan⁻¹.

5 Write it, then tune

Two different misses

Short / long → the blocks. Re-check c ÷ 27.

Left / right of the ball → the angle. Re-check tan⁻¹(across ÷ up), and that the turn-back matches.

The key coaching move

Separate the two errors. A wrong-distance team fixes blocks; a wrong-direction team fixes the angle. Mixing them is how teams go in circles. The launch snippet goes in STUDENT ZONE B of the match code; the locked part drives the last bit and kicks.

The one thing to test before the competition: does a bot, aimed and driven to the ball, actually kick it into the goal? Do a couple of dry launches yourself. Wide goal + short distance behind the ball makes this reliable. If the kick is flaky, widen the goal or move it closer — do not let a weak kick get blamed on the math.

6 The competition

It's fair by construction. Everyone launches from the same spot at the same ball, and everyone drives the exact distance from their own equation — so the winner has the sharpest math + aim, not the luckiest setup. Most goals out of 5; one neutral ref calls in/out. Bracket it if you want a champion.

7 Why it's really one rule

The point

A straight shot is across = 0 → tan⁻¹(0) = 0°. The 45° launch is across = up → tan⁻¹(1) = 45°. Same formula, special numbers.

What to draw out

Students often learn "straight shot" and "45° diagonal" as separate tricks. Show that both fall out of tan⁻¹(across ÷ up) — one rule for any ball position — so there is nothing extra to memorize. This is the whole idea of Strategy 3 as the general case.
