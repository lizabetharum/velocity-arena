Meet Maya

Maya is fourteen. She just finished eighth grade and starts high school in September — a new school, bigger and louder than the middle school she just left, in a building she has never set foot in before. Her parents enrolled her in this summer program at the new school because her older sister told them she would have loved this at her age. Her sister is going to college in the fall as a physics major. Maya loves her sister. Maya is not her sister.

Maya does not like math. Something stopped clicking around fifth grade and never started clicking again. Her last report card had a C+ in pre-algebra and she felt good about the plus. Algebra 1 is on her schedule for the fall — she is dreading it. She has spent the last four years making sure adults know she is "not a math kid," because if she names it first, no one else gets to name it for her. She is not afraid of numbers. She is afraid of being asked to be wrong in front of people.

On Day 1 she walks into a classroom in the high school she will attend in the fall — a room that does not look like a classroom. Robots on every small group table. The walls have a few areas taped off and labeled but empty — Leaderboard, Bot Names, Rivalry Cards — as if the room is waiting for something to happen in it. She has no idea what any of those words mean yet. The teacher waves everyone over and asks them to form a circle. Within five minutes Maya is standing in that circle, and the teacher is explaining how to play Circle Pass.

MATCH-BUILD-MAKE

Coordinate Navigation — Arc Approximation

The activity opens with a launch sequence that brings the morning's distance formula straight into the room. The arena is an 8×4 grid with the ball at (4, 2) — dead center. Teams pick one of the two starting corners: (0, 4) or (8, 0). Maya's team picks (0, 4). She does the math on her worksheet — d = √((4−0)² + (2−4)²) = √(16 + 4) = √20 ≈ 4.47 squares × 27 cm/square ≈ 121 cm. But the bot doesn't drive in cm — at SPEED = 6 it drives at a constant PWM for whatever number of milliseconds you tell it. So Maya does a second conversion. She flips back to her Day 1 Speed Stat Challenge notes: at SPEED = 6, Patience covers 1 cm every 33 ms (her measured calibration). Multiply: 121 × 33 ≈ 4000 ms. Iris programs Patience to cruise at 30% PWM for 4000 ms, then stop — using the Launch Sequence Quick Help page since she has never written a Cutebot program from scratch before. Patience drives. They measure: 8 cm short of the ball. Maya logs the gap. Before they move on the teacher says it once: "Save your launch sequence. Name it so you can find it. You will reuse this on Day 5." Iris hits the Share button in MakeCode, copies the URL, and pastes it into the team's notes — labeled LAUNCH-V1 · (0,4)→(4,2) · 121 cm · 4000 ms @ SPEED 6.

CHALLENGE

Power Stat Challenge — Ratio and Motor Speed

The formula goes on the board: Charge Speed % = Power². The teacher asks for a gut prediction for Power = 8 before anyone calculates. Maya writes 40 — she assumed it would be linear like Speed was, so eight times five. Then she does the math: 8² = 64. The teacher pushes the comparison: P = 4 gives 16. P = 8 gives 64. Doubling Power quadrupled charge speed. Maya looks down at the inside cover of her notebook, at the sentence she copied moments ago — faster ≠ better — and realizes this is exactly where the teacher told her to watch for it. More Power doesn't just give you more kick. It gives you a lot more kick. Possibly more kick than the ball needs.

The Explore phase uses three tools at once. Teams open the Power Stat Challenge interactive lesson on a device, which walks them through the ratio table and the underlying formula chargePower = POWER². Maya fills in the worksheet column by column — Power, Power², resulting chargePower — then logs a prediction for each before any bot moves. Iris downloads the Power Test program to the bot — Week 1 still has the three of them trading off who's at the iPad, and right now it's Iris's turn. They place the ball about 40 cm in front of the bot, set POWER = 2, and press Button A. The bot's ultrasonic sensor pings the ball, computes chargePower (4%), drives until it reaches the ball, strikes through it for 250 ms, then follows through for 190 ms. Maya measures the result. Reflash with POWER = 4. The chargePower jumps to 16 and the follow-through stretches to 260 ms — Patience arrives faster now, with a firm bump that pushes the ball forward maybe a foot before it stops.
