Meet Maya

Maya is fourteen. She just finished eighth grade and starts high school in September — a new school, bigger and louder than the middle school she just left, in a building she has never set foot in before. Her parents enrolled her in this summer program at the new school because her older sister told them she would have loved this at her age. Her sister is going to college in the fall as a physics major. Maya loves her sister. Maya is not her sister.

Maya does not like math. Something stopped clicking around fifth grade and never started clicking again. Her last report card had a C+ in pre-algebra and she felt good about the plus. Algebra 1 is on her schedule for the fall — she is dreading it. She has spent the last four years making sure adults know she is "not a math kid," because if she names it first, no one else gets to name it for her. She is not afraid of numbers. She is afraid of being asked to be wrong in front of people.

On Day 1 she walks into a classroom in the high school she will attend in the fall — a room that does not look like a classroom. Robots on every small group table. The walls have a few areas taped off and labeled but empty — Leaderboard, Bot Names, Rivalry Cards — as if the room is waiting for something to happen in it. She has no idea what any of those words mean yet. The teacher waves everyone over and asks them to form a circle. Within five minutes Maya is standing in that circle, and the teacher is explaining how to play Circle Pass.

DAY 01

Speed · Welcome · The First Bot Drive

Week 1 · Play Lab + Boot Camp
Maya's arc today: Outsider in a room of strangers → has a team → has touched a robot → has done one piece of real math in front of people who didn't laugh.
HOOK

Sound and Movement: Circle Pass + Mirror Pairs

When Maya's turn arrives she claps once and lifts her left foot. The girl to her left copies it exactly, then adds a snap. By the third lap around the circle the room is loud and uncoordinated and nobody is performing — they are just doing it. In Mirror Pairs she gets matched with a boy named Devon who moves slowly enough that she can keep up; she moves slower for him on the swap. Nobody mentions math. Nothing is graded. Maya notices that her shoulders are no longer at her ears.

She has not said her name yet, but she has been copied by another person three times. That counts as something.
HOOK

Name + One Word

The teacher asks everyone back into the circle. "Go around. Say your name and one word that describes how you feel right now." Maya can hear other kids saying things like "excited" and "good" and "ready." She does not feel those things. She is third from the end. By the time her turn comes she has decided to tell the truth.

What Maya said out loud — first time in the room
"Maya. Tired."

The teacher nods once and moves on without commenting. Nobody laughs. Nobody asks why. Maya realizes she just said her own name in front of nineteen strangers and survived.

Speed Stat Challenge — What Does Motor Power Actually Mean?

The formula goes on the board: Motor Power % = Speed × 100 ÷ 20. The teacher asks the class what 100% means in everyday life. A boy says "phone battery." A girl says "health bar in a video game." The teacher nods and points to the formula. Before anyone calculates anything, every student writes a prediction for Speed = 10. Maya writes 50% — not because she did the formula, but because 10 is half of 20, and half of 100 is 50. She treats the answer like a guess. She is right and assumes she got lucky.

The teacher directs the students to a website and a webpage with the speed activity. They are told to work on their own devices, but that teams can work together.

MATCH-BUILD-MAKE

First Bot Drive — Speed in Real Life

The central question on the board and on the next webpage: does doubling your Speed double the distance the bot travels in 2 seconds? Before anyone touches a robot, Maya circles "yes — proportional" on her worksheet, because that's what the formula says. She does the same on the webpage. Then Iris uploads the code, Devon hits go, and they measure with masking tape and a tape measure — a starting line, a finish mark, and the actual cm read off the tape. At Speed = 3, the bot moves 18 cm in 3 seconds. At Speed = 6, it moves 44.6 cm. Maya wrote down 36 as the prediction. The actual is almost 9 cm farther. The formula was wrong. Or the bot was. Maya looks up from the worksheet and notices, against her will, that she wants to know which.

Free Code Time

DAY 02

Endurance · Decay · Naming the Bot

Week 1 · Play Lab + Boot Camp
Maya's arc today: Makes a mistake on purpose in front of 19 people and gets cheered for it → graphs her first real linear decay → has a role (Analyst) → has a bot with a name.
HOOK

Failure Bow

The teacher goes first: she trips on purpose, takes an exaggerated bow, and the room erupts. Real or fake, big or small, doesn't matter. Maya's turn is fourth. She drops her pencil on purpose, bends to pick it up, and gives a very small bow. The room cheers anyway — loud, generous, not at her, with her. Something inside her unclenches one click.

She made a mistake on purpose and was rewarded for it. The shield she has been carrying for four years has a crack in it that wasn't there yesterday.
HOOK

Slow Down, Slow Down

The teacher calls a tempo and the room matches it. Slower. Slower. Slower than Maya thought a group of 18 teenagers could go. By the time they're moving in slow motion she can hear the fan.

By the Explore slide she is plugging her team's E = 4 into the same formula on paper. On the Lock In slide she writes Endurance = 4 on her stat card and confirms.

Bot Naming Ceremony — Generated by the Math

The teacher opens the Robot Team Name Generator on the projector and says one sentence: "The math will generate your bot's name. Not random — determined." The I-Do takes four minutes. The We-Do walks the whole class through sample inputs x = 4, y = 3, z = 2, working three formulas in sequence — a = 2x + y − 1, b = (a + z) mod 12, n = 3z² − x — and landing on Kinetic Falcons #8. This is where Maya catches both gotchas: she almost writes 2x as 2 + x, then catches herself; and when the teacher writes 3z² on the board she has to remember it means 3 × (z²), not (3z)². Then it is the You-Do, and every student plugs in their own values. Maya works on paper before touching the tool: x = 4 (M-A-Y-A), y = 5 (May), z = 3 (their team number). While she is double-checking, she notices Devon writing (3 × 3)² = 81 next to her arm. She does not say anything. She just slides her paper toward him with the order of operations written out. He looks, says "oh," and re-does it. They post all three of the team's calculated names on the wall. Iris reads them aloud. Maya's name is "Patient Foxes #23". Iris says, "Patient Foxes. I like it. DAY 03

Turning · Coordinates · The Distance Formula

Week 1 · Play Lab + Boot Camp
Maya's arc today: Learns that natural language can't program a robot, learns the distance formula, walks an arc with her own feet, and watches Patience trace the pattern her math predicted.
HOOK

Human Robot Programming Challenge

Pairs of students. One plays robot. The other gives instructions. Maya is the robot first; Devon directs her. "Move forward three steps. Turn 90 degrees left. Move forward two steps." She does — but each "step" she takes is a different size, and the 90-degree turn ends up at maybe 70 degrees, and she lands a foot off where Devon wanted her. They swap. Maya is more specific. "Move forward, heel-to-toe, four times. Pivot left until your shoulders face the door." Devon ends up closer. Still not exact.

Words are not precise enough to drive a robot. That is why formulas exist. Maya did not know that was a problem formulas solved.
CHALLENGE

Distance-Time Graphs + The Distance Formula

The teacher walks by, taps the paper, and says one word: "Why?" Maya looks again. The slope is shallower. She rewrites: "Run B's Speed allocation was lower — the slope is smaller, so it travels less distance per second." Then comes the distance formula on the arena grid. MATCH-BUILD-MAKE

Coordinate Navigation — Arc Approximation

The activity opens with a launch sequence that brings the morning's distance formula straight into the room. Teams pick a corner of the arena; the ball sits at (6, 4) on the grid. Maya's team is at (1, 1). She does the math on her worksheet — d = √((6−1)² + (4−1)²) = √(25 + 9) = √34 ≈ 5.83 squares — and Iris programs Patience to drive that exact distance straight at the ball. Patience drives. They measure: 8 cm short. Maya logs the gap.

Then the embodied piece. Maya walks a 2-segment "arc" to a target spot (it looks like a triangle, not a curve), then walks an 8-segment one (it looks like a curve, almost). She gets that more segments approximate a curve but never equal one.

Back at the worksheet — before anyone reveals the formula — teams calculate turnRatio for T = 5: 0.2 + 5 × 0.02 = 0.30. Maya draws three arc paths on the grid for T = 2, 5, and 10, predicting where each will land. Only then does the teacher turn to the board and write the formula they all just used: turnRatio = 0.2 + T × 0.02. Maya already had it on her paper.

Now the MakeCode part. The team opens the Turning Test program. The defaults all start at zero — SPEED, ENDURANCE, TURNING — and the teacher says one thing: "If any stat is zero, the bot won't move. Fill them in from your stat card before you flash." Iris (Coder this week) types in SPEED = 6, ENDURANCE = 4. Then they cycle through the Coverage Challenge: TURNING = 2, flash, place Patience on the start, press Button A. The bot roams for 15 seconds tracing gentle, long arcs; the RGB strip on top alternates blue when arcing left and green when arcing right. Maya tallies squares covered on the taped grid. Reflash with TURNING = 5. Reflash with TURNING = 10. With each higher T, the arcs get sharper and shorter. At T = 10, Patience hits the white border — and Maya watches it brake, back up, turn 115°, and keep going without anyone touching it. It recovered itself.

DAY 04

Power · The Quadratic Surprise · Build a Scoop

Week 1 · Play Lab + Boot Camp
Maya's arc today: Discovers that not all formulas are lines — Power² breaks her intuition wide open. Then her hands build something that kicks a ball.
HOOK

Finger Speed-Sums — Faster ≠ Better

Students stand in a circle. The teacher gives the rules: hold up some number of fingers; the goal is to call the group total first. Each round speeds up. Round 1 is slow — Maya counts everyone's fingers carefully, gets seven, and someone else calls it. Round 2 she sums faster and wins clean. Round 3 the tempo doubles; Maya shouts "twelve" before she has actually counted — the real total is thirteen. She loses the round to someone who took half a second longer. By round 5, she has noticed the pattern: when she pushed for speed, her error rate went up. Iris laughs. Maya laughs. Then the teacher pulls the class into the debrief: "When did being fast help you? When did it hurt? Where else in your life have you seen this pattern?" The class names sports, video games, music practice. The teacher writes one sentence on the board and leaves it there for the rest of the day: faster ≠ better. Maya copies it onto the inside cover of her journal. "Save this thought," the teacher says. "Power is next. Watch for where this shows up."

CHALLENGE

Power Stat Challenge — Ratio and Motor Speed

The formula goes on the board: Charge Speed % = Power². The teacher asks for a gut prediction for Power = 8 before anyone calculates. Maya writes 40 — she assumed it would be linear like Speed was, so eight times five. Then she does the math: 8² = 64. The teacher pushes the comparison: P = 4 gives 16. P = 8 gives 64. Doubling Power quadrupled charge speed. Maya looks down at the inside cover of her notebook, at the sentence she copied moments ago — faster ≠ better — and realizes this is exactly where the teacher told her to watch for it. More Power doesn't just give you more kick. It gives you a lot more kick. Possibly more kick than the ball needs.

The Explore phase uses three tools at once. Teams open the Power Stat Challenge interactive lesson on a device, which walks them through the ratio table and the underlying formula chargePower = motorPower + Power². Maya fills in the worksheet column by column — Power, Power², resulting chargePower for her team's SPEED = 6 — then logs a prediction for each before any bot moves. Iris (Coder) downloads the Power Test program to the bot. They place the ball about 40 cm in front of the bot, set POWER = 2, and press Button A. The bot's ultrasonic sensor pings the ball, computes chargePower (30 + 4 = 34), drives the measured distance, and follows through for 190 ms. Maya measures the result. Reflash with POWER = 4. The chargePower jumps to 46 and the follow-through stretches to 260 ms — Patience arrives faster now, with a firm bump that pushes the ball forward maybe a foot before it stops. Reflash with POWER = 8. The chargePower jumps to 94 (almost maxed) and the follow-through stretches to 400 ms. Patience races toward the ball at full PWM and slams into it. The bot stops exactly where the code told it to — at the ball — and the ball rolls forward just a couple of feet before stopping. Maya looks at the numbers: she just spent 64 percentage points of Power² to move the ball about twice as far as POWER = 4 did. Iris is impressed by the speed of the charge. Maya is more interested in the math: at POWER = 8 the bot moved 16× the Power² of POWER = 2, but the ball moved maybe 4× the distance. That's the diminishing return — without something to channel the impact, a bare bumper can only transfer so much energy to a ball. The formula's prediction is mathematically right; the bot just runs out of useful work to do.

Above a certain Power value, more chargePower stops paying off. Possibly Power = 4 is the sweet spot for our team. Need to test more before locking it in. That stat point might be better applied somewhere else. (Maybe with a scoop the curve changes — building one next.)"
