Failure Mode Library — Training Resource

What bad facilitation
actually looks like.

You can read about ideal facilitation all day. You learn it faster by seeing the breakdown — the exact moment things go wrong, why it happens, and what it should look like instead. Every example below is based on real failure patterns in programs like this.

Core Insight from Reviewer
"Facilitator execution variance is where programs like this usually fail. You are trying to engineer culture through facilitation behavior. That is much harder than teaching robotics."
Failure Mode 01
Accepting Vague Answers
Happens during: Challenge Block, Match debrief, any discussion. Most common in Days 1–4.
✗ Breakdown Facilitator lets the claim stand
Student
"Our bot is really fast. We think Speed is the most important stat."
Facilitator
"Great point! Speed is definitely important. What else did everyone notice?"
Different student
"Yeah, their bot was fast too."
Facilitator
"Exactly. Okay, let's move on to Endurance."
What went wrong
"Our bot is fast" was accepted as a complete thought. The facilitator validated it, amplified it, and moved on. No formula. No number. No trade-off. The standard "every claim needs a number" was abandoned on Day 2. Now you'll fight it for 14 more days.
✓ Correct Facilitator presses for the number
Student
"Our bot is really fast. We think Speed is the most important stat."
Facilitator
"What's your Speed value? And what does that give you for Motor%?"
Student
"S is 12. So... Motor% is 60."
Facilitator
"Okay. And what does a team lose when they allocate 12 to Speed? What's not getting those points?"
What's different
The facilitator didn't accept the vague claim. Pressed once for a number. Got it. Then immediately redirected to the trade-off. Two moves. The standard is held without confrontation.
⚡
The Pattern to Break
Every time you accept "I think" or "it feels like" or "it's good" — you train students that vague answers are acceptable. Do it once and the class sets its own standard. Press for evidence every single time in Days 1–5 and you almost never have to in Days 6–16.
Failure Mode 02
Rescuing the Silence Early
Happens during: Any question asked to the full group. Worse in Days 1–3 before norms are established.
✗ Breakdown Facilitator fills the silence at 3 seconds
Facilitator
"If S = 10, what's Motor%? ... [2 seconds] ... No one? Okay, let me rephrase. If Speed equals 10 and the max is 20, what percent of the maximum is that? ... [1 second] ... It's 50%. Motor% = 50%."
Students
[Write down 50% and wait for the next question.]
What went wrong
Facilitator asked, waited 2 seconds, rephrased, waited 1 more second, then answered their own question. Students learned: if you wait, you don't have to think. The facilitator trained learned helplessness in one exchange. This pattern compounds across every Challenge Block.
✓ Correct Facilitator holds the gap to 7 seconds, then deploys Turn and Talk
Facilitator
"If S = 10, what's Motor%?"
[Silence — 7 seconds. Facilitator scans. No hands.]
...counting internally, not moving, not nodding...
Facilitator
"Take 90 seconds. Tell your partner: what formula gets you Motor%, and what's the answer when S is 10."
[After Turn and Talk]
"We got 50%. It's 10 divided by 20, times 100."
What's different
Silence held. No rephrasing. At 7 seconds with no hands, Turn and Talk — not a repeat of the question. Students did the thinking. The facilitator collected the answer, not produced it.
⚡
Why This Is Hard
7 seconds of silence in front of 15 students feels like 7 minutes. You feel responsible for their discomfort. You're not. That discomfort is distributed thinking — don't resolve it. The moment you rephrase, you signal that the silence was a problem instead of a process.
Failure Mode 03
Over-Scaffolding the Formula
Happens during: Challenge Block instruction. Most common when facilitator is anxious about time or student confusion.
✗ Breakdown Facilitator walks students through every step before they try
Facilitator
"Okay, so for Motor%, here's how you do it. Take your S value — let's say it's 8 — and you divide it by 20. So 8 ÷ 20 is 0.4. Then you multiply by 100 to get the percent. So 0.4 × 100 = 40%. Motor% is 40. Got it? Now you try with your S value."
Student
"My S is 10... so 10 divided by 20 is 0.5... times 100 is 50."
Facilitator
"Perfect! You all got it."
What went wrong
The facilitator modeled the entire procedure before students attempted anything. The "try" phase was just copying the steps with a different number. Students learned a procedure. They did not learn why the formula works, what percent means, or how to apply this when the parameters change in Week 3.
✓ Correct Facilitator asks before modeling
Facilitator
"Motor% tells you what percent of your motor's capacity you're using. If S = 10 and the max is 20 — what percent is that? Don't calculate yet. Just think about what 10 out of 20 means."
Student
"It's half. 50%."
Facilitator
"Right. And the formula just makes that explicit: S ÷ 20 × 100. Why divide by 20? What does that represent?"
Student
"Because 20 is the maximum Speed value?"
Facilitator
"Exactly. Now calculate Motor% for your own S value."
What's different
Meaning before procedure. The student explained the formula's logic before being given steps. That understanding transfers when parameters change. Procedure-only learning doesn't.
Failure Mode 04
Incomplete Loss Recovery
Happens during: Match-Build-Make after any team loss. Most damaging on Day 7 (first loss) and Day 10 (Season 1 Final).
✗ Breakdown Facilitator skips Step 1 to get to the analysis
Student [immediately after loss]
"This is so stupid. Our bot literally did nothing. I don't want to do this anymore."
Facilitator
"I know it's frustrating — let's look at what we can learn from this. Open your Match Log. What did you predict?"
Student
[stares at the table, doesn't open the log]
Facilitator
"Come on, this is useful data. Your allocation might just need a small adjustment..."
What went wrong
The facilitator moved directly to analysis — "let's look at what we can learn" — while the student was still in the emotional spike. The student didn't hear any of it. Step 1 (acknowledge, sit with it, 30 seconds of silence) was completely skipped. Pushing analysis onto an unresolved emotional state produces resistance, not reflection.
✓ Correct Step 1 first. Always.
Student [immediately after loss]
"This is so stupid. Our bot literally did nothing. I don't want to do this anymore."
Facilitator
[sits down next to the team. Makes eye contact. Says nothing for ~25 seconds.]
Student
[breathing slows slightly] "...it just didn't move the way we coded it."
Facilitator
"Open the Match Log. What did you predict would happen before the match?"
What's different
Silence first. No explanation, no redirect, no "I know it's frustrating." Just presence. The student resolved the spike enough to hear Step 2. The whole exchange took 40 seconds longer than the bad version — and it actually worked.
⚡
The Instinct to Fight
Your instinct is to be helpful — to redirect the pain into productivity. That's the wrong order. Emotional regulation comes before cognitive engagement. You can't skip Step 1 and get Step 3. Run the protocol in sequence, every time, even when it feels slow.
Failure Mode 05
Identity-Triggering Phrasing
Happens during: Pre-diagnostic (Day 1), Cold Recall (Day 10), post-diagnostic (Day 15), any comparison moment. Often unintentional.
You Say This Student Hears This Say This Instead
"This should be easy — it's just percentages." "If I don't get it, I'm worse than other students." "This is a percent calculation. Take a minute and try it."
"You already know how to do this from school." "If I don't remember, I failed at school." "Some of you may have seen this before. Start with what you know."
"Who got this?" [scanning the room] "I am being evaluated on whether I raise my hand." "Take 90 seconds with your partner. Tell each other what you got."
"Does everyone understand?" [to full group] "If I say no, I'm admitting I'm behind." "Tell me one thing that still feels unclear — write it, don't say it yet."
"How did everyone do on the diagnostic?" [publicly] "My score is about to become public." Don't ask this. Collect individually. Results are between you and each student.
"Team A did great — let's all learn from them." "I am being ranked against Team A." "Team A made an interesting choice — what data led them there? Can you ask them?"
"This is your chance to show what you've learned." "This is a performance. My learning is being evaluated right now." "This is the same as Day 1 — information for the program, not a grade for you."
⚡
How Identity Threat Works
You don't have to say anything overtly negative to trigger academic identity threat. The phrase "this should be easy" is enough. Once a student believes they're being evaluated on a fixed trait (ability), they protect the ego by not trying. Phrases that create comparison, imply prior knowledge, or frame performance as public produce this effect. It's not about intention — it's about what students hear.
Failure Mode 06
Bad Debrief — Match Without Data
Happens during: Post-match discussion in Match-Build-Make. Any match day, Days 7–15.
✗ Breakdown Debrief focuses on winning, not data
Facilitator [after match]
"Great match, everyone! Team Apex really showed up today. What did people notice about how the bots moved?"
Student
"Team Apex was way faster."
Facilitator
"Yeah, they had great Speed. What else? Did anyone notice the turning?"
Student
"Their turns were tighter."
Facilitator
"Exactly! Good observations. Okay, next match."
What went wrong
No gap sentence. No Match Log. No formula reference. No prediction versus actual. The "debrief" was an oral recap of impressions — "faster," "tighter" — with no numbers. Five minutes of conversation produced zero data. Teams have nothing to take into the next match.
✓ Correct Debrief anchored to the Match Log and gap sentence
Facilitator [after match]
"Open your Match Log. What did you predict before this match? What happened? And what's the gap — give me a number."
Student
"We predicted 2 goals. We scored 0. Gap is 2."
Facilitator
"What decision connects most directly to that gap? One decision."
Student
"Our Motor% was 40 — we thought that was enough but the field is bigger than we thought."
Facilitator
"What would you test differently? Finish this: if we change ___, we predict ___."
What's different
Match Log open. Prediction stated. Actual stated. Gap computed. One decision identified. Hypothesis formed. The team leaves with something testable. That's a debrief.
Failure Mode 07
Commissioner's Meeting — Over-Facilitation
Happens during: Day 12 Commissioner's Meeting. The most common facilitation mistake on this specific day.
✗ Breakdown Facilitator improves the proposal in real time
Team presenting proposal
"We think the underdog team should get 3 extra points instead of 2, because 2 isn't enough."
Facilitator
"Interesting — but have you thought about what happens to the competitive balance? Maybe you could add a constraint, like the 3 extra points can only go to specific stats?"
Student
"Oh yeah, that's a good idea. We can add that."
Facilitator
"Okay, so the proposal is 3 extra points with a stat restriction. Does that make it stronger?"
What went wrong
The facilitator added content to the proposal. The "stat restriction" idea came from the facilitator, not the team. Now the class is voting on something the facilitator designed — not a student-authored rule change. The community ownership is gone. The facilitator also cued the class that weak proposals need improving, so teams will look to them for help instead of the data.
✓ Correct Facilitator holds the format, not the content
Team presenting proposal
"We think the underdog team should get 3 extra points instead of 2, because 2 isn't enough."
Facilitator
"Does your proposal cite two dashboard numbers as required?"
Student
"Um... not really."
Facilitator
"The proposal requirement is two dashboard numbers. You can add them now, or present as-is and the class will evaluate. Your choice."
Student [during class debate later]
"But they didn't show any data."
Facilitator
"That's a valid point for the vote. Anyone else before we vote?"
What's different
The facilitator enforced the format requirement (two dashboard numbers) without improving the proposal. The class debate exposed the weakness. The class voted — and they own the outcome, not the facilitator.
⚡
Why This Is Hard
You can see the proposal is weak. You know a better version exists. Your instinct is to help. But the moment you improve a proposal, you own it — not the student. The class debate is where learning happens. A weak proposal that gets rejected in a community vote teaches more about evidence-based reasoning than a strong proposal that the facilitator co-wrote.
Failure Mode 08
Rushing the Bot Naming Ceremony
Happens during: Day 2, Hook and Match-Build-Make. Often caused by time pressure and not recognizing the activity's function.
✗ Breakdown Facilitator treats naming as a logistics task
Facilitator
"Okay teams, quick — pick a name for your bot. You've got 3 minutes. It doesn't have to be anything fancy, just something to identify you on the leaderboard."
Student A
"Just call it Team 4."
Student B
"Fine."
Facilitator [3 minutes later]
"Okay, what did everyone come up with? Let me write them down."
What went wrong
"It doesn't have to be anything fancy" told students the activity was low-stakes. "Just something to identify you on the leaderboard" made it a logistics task. One student checked out and said "Team 4." The other agreed. No discussion. No ownership. That team will change strategy the moment results are hard — they have no emotional investment in the bot.
✓ Correct Facilitator treats naming as identity formation
Facilitator
"This name is permanent. It goes on the wall today and stays there until Day 16. It's how we'll talk about your bot in matches, on the leaderboard, and in every gap sentence. Take the time to get it right. What's your bot's story? What does the name mean?"
Students [10 minutes of debate later]
"We're calling it Voltcore. Because we want maximum Power but we also have high Endurance — like a volt that never dies."
Facilitator
"Voltcore. [Writes it on the wall immediately.] That name just got permanent. Remember it — I'll be calling this bot by name for 15 more days."
What's different
Permanence stated upfront. Story required. Ten minutes of debate happened — that was team ownership forming in real time. The name was connected to their allocation strategy before they even submitted Day 5's numbers. That team will work harder to defend Voltcore's design through Week 3 losses.
Quick Reference
Facilitation Failure Signals
Use this to self-diagnose during or after a session. If you spot a failure signal, check the corresponding failure mode above.
You Notice This Likely Failure Mode Immediate Correction
Students using "it feels like" or "our bot is good" with no numbers Failure Mode 01 — Vague answers accepted Stop. Ask: "What's the formula output for that? Give me a number."
You rephrased a question before 7 seconds elapsed Failure Mode 02 — Silence rescued early Next question: ask once, hold silence to 7 sec, then Turn and Talk.
Students can calculate the formula but can't explain why it works Failure Mode 03 — Over-scaffolded instruction Next session: ask "why does this formula work?" before modeling the procedure.
Team is still in emotional shutdown 6+ minutes after a loss Failure Mode 04 — Loss Recovery skipped or rushed Run Step 1 now — sit, silence, no explanation. Don't move to the log until the emotional spike visibly drops.
Students disengaged during diagnostics or refusing to try Failure Mode 05 — Identity-triggering phrasing Reframe immediately: "This is information for the program, not a grade for you."
Post-match discussion is opinions and impressions, no numbers Failure Mode 06 — Bad debrief Stop. "Open the Match Log. Prediction. Actual. Gap. Give me a number."
You suggested an improvement to a Commissioner's Meeting proposal Failure Mode 07 — Over-facilitation Stop improving. Return to format enforcement: "Does this proposal cite two dashboard numbers?"
Teams with no emotional investment in their bot name by Day 3 Failure Mode 08 — Bot Naming rushed Reference bot names in every session. Ask teams what their bot's name means. Rebuild investment through language.
