Formula Reference
Charge Speed %  =  Power²
Power 2 → 4% Power 4 → 16% Power 8 → 64%
When Power doubles → charge speed quadruples. Real-world distance may not.
Before you build or test — make a prediction.
If charge speed increases 4× (from 16% to 64%), the ball will travel: 4 × as far.
Circle your reasoning:
exactly 4× — charge speed scales directly to distance
less than 4× — real-world losses reduce the output
more than 4× — the scoop adds extra force
Explain how you got your prediction (1 sentence): if charge speed is 4× higher the ball should go 4× farther because it has 4× more power

Teacher Look-For — Approaching (Marty T.)
Marty correctly computed both charge speeds (4²=16, 8²=64) — the squaring formula is not the problem. His error is in the ratio step: instead of dividing 64÷16=4, he sees that Power doubled (4→8) and writes that as the charge speed ratio: 2×. Because his distance ratio (88÷40=2.2) is also about 2×, he concludes charge speed and distance "matched" — the exact opposite of what the data shows. The formula reference right on page 1 says "when Power doubles → charge speed quadruples," which is the direct check he bypassed. Ask him: "You wrote 64÷16 and got 2. Can you do that division again step by step? What's 64 divided by 16?"

Teacher Look-For — Exceeds (Priya R.)
Priya correctly computed the 4× charge speed ratio, identified the mismatch (2.3× distance), and named a precise cause (energy converts to heat and compression at contact). She then read the efficiency section against the ratio analysis — noting that even though charge speed and distance don't scale proportionally, efficiency per power point still goes UP, which means the energy loss doesn't eliminate the advantage of higher Power. That move (reconciling two conflicting signals from the data) is the key analytical skill. Her final decision to choose Power 6 — rather than 4 or 8 — uses the efficiency trend as evidence and names the budget trade-off explicitly (8 pts = 40% of budget). The phrase "based on the trend it should be better per point" shows appropriate uncertainty about an untested value, which is exactly the epistemic voice this sheet is reaching for.
