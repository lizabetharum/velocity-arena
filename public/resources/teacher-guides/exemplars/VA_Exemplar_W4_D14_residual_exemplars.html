Standard: S, M, L → getting bigger as opp Speed ↑. Chaos: S, M, M → same trend. Fanning!
Step 2 — Sorting question: did the direction flip?
No — too high in both modes, same direction. Pattern held across both balls.
Step 3 — Size pattern
S → M → L in Standard. S → M → M in Chaos. Growing both times = fanning. Points to m.
Step 4 — Change one value + two sentences
The miss grew every time the opponent got faster — S near weak opponents, L against fast ones — in both ball modes.
S→M→L means the line's angle is wrong, not its starting point. Near weaker opponents we were close. Against strong ones the gap opened. Changing b would just shift the whole line — the fanning would still be there. We need to steepen m so the prediction drops faster as opponents get faster.
How much? Standard actual: 60 → 52 → 44, drops 4/Speed unit. Our slope: −1/unit. Gap ≈ 3/unit. New m = −1 − 3 = −4. (estimate! Match Day 3 tests it)
Changing m from −1 to −4.  Revised: y = −4x + 65
Step 5 — Match Day 3 hypothesis
Based on S→M→L fanning in both modes, I predict ~41% vs opp Speed 6. y = −4(6) + 65 = 41%. The −4 came from the actual drop rate in Standard (4 pts/unit vs our 1 pt/unit), so it's an estimate. Match Day 3 will tell us if −4 is close enough or if we need to adjust again.
Sample — pre-pilot
Teacher look-for
Priya read the fanning pattern correctly across both ball modes, matched it to m rather than b, and showed her reasoning for why changing b would leave the problem intact. Her Step 4 includes the derivation: actual drop rate (4/unit) minus model drop rate (1/unit) gives the correction (3), so m goes to −4. She explicitly labeled the new slope as an estimate and named what Match Day 3 tests. That is residual analysis: a structural diagnosis, one targeted change, and appropriate uncertainty attached to the inference.
