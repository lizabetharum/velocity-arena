Distance Formula: Shortest Path to the Goal

Students use the distance formula to measure how far their ball traveled (A→B) and how far it still needs to go (B→G), then compare "total movement" to "useful progress." The low sample writes raw differences in the Add step instead of squared values — a one-place error that cascades into an impossible efficiency greater than 1.00 (more progress than movement). The high sample uses efficiency=1.00 to argue that the bottleneck is Power, not aim. Illustrative samples, built before the pilot.

Teacher look-for
Marty squares each difference correctly (2²=4, 1²=1) but in the Add step he writes the raw differences (2+1=3) instead of the squares (4+1=5). This cascades: A→B=√3≈1.73, B→G=√1=1. His efficiency (3÷1.73≈1.73) is mathematically impossible — you cannot reduce distance by more than you traveled. His Big Idea sentence shows this impossibility directly. This is his consistent pattern: individual pieces computed right, binding rule missed. Ask: "In your Add step, where did the 2 and 1 come from? Check your squares from the lines above."
