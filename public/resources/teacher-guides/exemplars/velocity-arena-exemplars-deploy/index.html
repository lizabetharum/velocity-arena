How to Read These Exemplars

Each activity shows three levels of student work. The right column names the specific thinking pattern to look for — useful during circulating, discussion, and reviewing written work.

Approaching

Student attempts the task but makes a procedural, conceptual, or reasoning error. The error is specific and addressable — not a random guess. This is the most common state for students encountering a new concept.

Meets

Student applies the formula or concept correctly and shows work. The calculation is accurate. The explanation connects the number to bot behavior, though often brief or literal.

Exceeds

Student goes beyond correct calculation: they notice a pattern, make a strategic connection, anticipate a consequence, or explain the trade-off. The math is correct and the thinking is transferable.

These are artificial examples — not transcribed from real students. They are intentionally imperfect at the lower levels.

Floor rule: Bots never drop below 20% motor power during matches — the decay formula predicts a theoretical zero-crossing, but in play the bot slows to 20% and stays there.

Follow-through formula: Power × 80 ms (not 100 ms).

Week 1 — Pre-Season: Stat Boot Camp (Days 1–4)

Students build intuition for each stat formula before connecting to match outcomes. Goal: calculation fluency and the first link between formula output and physical behavior.

Approaching
"When we doubled Speed, distance also doubled. It works."
Makes a claim without citing measured data.
Teacher Look-For: Student asserts 'it worked' without using a distance ratio. Prompt: 'What two numbers from your table support that?'
Meets

Teacher Look-For: Uses real data, calculates ratio ≈ 2.48, and offers a physical explanation (friction threshold at low speeds) that goes beyond the formula.

Endurance table calculation + graph + comparison sentence (E = 4)
Approaching

▼
Student explanation of unit rate to a visitor (goals per Speed point) at the Community Exhibition station
Approaching
"Our unit rate is 0.5. It means for every Speed point you get 0.5 goals."
Correct definition but not connected to their actual data. Visitor cannot verify it.
Teacher Look-For: Uses unit rate vocabulary correctly but without data grounding. Ask: 'Can you show the division in your data log?'
Meets
"We scored 3 goals in Season 1 with Speed 6. Our unit rate is 3/6 = 0.5 goals per Speed point. We then changed to Speed 8 in Season 2. If the rate held, we'd predict 4 goals. We got 5 — slightly better than predicted."
Teacher Look-For: Calculates unit rate from actual data, makes a prediction, checks against Season 2 actual. Strong demonstration of mathematical reasoning.
Exceeds
"Our Season 1 unit rate: 3 goals / Speed 6 = 0.5 goals per point. Season 2: Speed 8. Predicted: 4 goals. Actual: 5. The rate slightly improved. Here's why: in Season 2 we also increased Turning, which helped in Chaos Mode. The unit rate captures Speed's contribution but the other stats also matter — it is only one variable in our win equation. [Shows visitor the full equation.] The rate tells part of the story."
Teacher Look-For: Explains the unit rate, identifies its limitation (one variable among many), and connects it to the linear equation — graduate-level statistical thinking in plain language.
