Season 2 Win Prediction — Voltage
Monitoring Chart Classification
Data-Anchored (surface) Marcus cites a Season 1 number, which looks like evidence. The error is in how he uses it — additive instead of proportional. Share 1st in discussion; the flaw will surface when pressed for the math.
Team · Marcus T.
We will win 6 of 9 matches.   Season 1 record: 3 wins / 6 games Season 2 = 3 more games than S1 3 wins + 3 more games = 6    3 + 3 = 6 ✓ also power went up so should be fine
Sample — pre-pilot
Teacher Look-For · Marcus T. · Approaching
Marcus cites Season 1 data (3 wins / 6 games) — a real number, correctly recalled. His error is in the combining step: he adds the extra Season 2 games to his Season 1 win total (3 + 3 = 6) instead of applying his win rate to the new match count (0.50 × 9 = 4.5 → 5). This is proportional reasoning used additively — his recurring pattern of getting individual pieces right but mishandling the operation that links them. Ask him: "If Season 2 had 18 games instead of 9, would you predict 3 + 12 = 15 wins? Does adding games to wins make sense as a unit?" That question usually breaks the error open immediately.

Season 2 Win Prediction — Circuit Breakers
Monitoring Chart Classification
Data-Anchored (genuine) Cites win rate, shows the multiplication, rounds. Share 1st in discussion — this sets the evidence standard for the room.
Team · Jayda W.
We will win 6 of 9 matches.   S1 record: 4 wins out of 6 games win rate = 4 ÷ 6 = 0.67 0.67 × 9 = 6.03 → round to 6 stats same as S1 → expect same rate
Sample — pre-pilot
Teacher Look-For · Jayda W. · Meets
Jayda correctly converts Season 1 wins to a win rate (4/6 = 0.67), multiplies by the Season 2 total (0.67 × 9 = 6.03), and rounds. She notes that stats are unchanged, which is the right factor to check. Her work is complete, correctly computed, and stops exactly at the math — she describes what the data says without connecting it to what it means for how her team should play tomorrow.

Season 2 Win Prediction — Overclock
Monitoring Chart Classification
Data-Anchored + Stat Change Reasoning Uses win rate and applies the stat formulas to explain why the number adjusts. Share 2nd or 3rd — after the base rate teams have modeled correct procedure, Priya shows what adding allocation analysis looks like.
Team · Priya R.
We will win 7 of 9 matches.   S1: 4/6 → rate = 0.67 → base: 0.67×9 = ~6 ↑ Speed 8→10: Motor Power 40%→50% ↓ Endurance 5→3: decay rate 15→17/min speed wins early, decay hurts late most matches < 4 min → speed wins → +1 → 7
Sample — pre-pilot
Teacher Look-For · Priya R. · Exceeds
Priya applies the rate correctly (0.67 × 9 ≈ 6) and then does something none of the other prediction types do: she uses the Season 1 stat formulas to quantify what her allocation change actually means. Speed 8 → 10 increases Motor Power from 40% to 50% (S × 5); Endurance 5 → 3 increases the decay rate from 15 to 17 (20 − E). She names the trade-off explicitly — faster start vs. shorter power window — and makes a reasoned call that match length favors her speed gain. That move from "we changed our stats" to "here is what that does to our rate by formula" is the mathematical practice the Teacher Content Note calls quantitative reasoning.
⚠ Assumption in these exemplars: Season 1 = 6 matches; Season 2 = 9 matches; Voltage 3/6, Circuit Breakers 4/6, Overclock 4/6. Stats unchanged for all three teams. Adjust if your actual class records differ — the quality of reasoning (not the specific numbers) is what these exemplars demonstrate.
