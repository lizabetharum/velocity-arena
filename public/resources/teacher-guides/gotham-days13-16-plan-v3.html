Gotham Tech · Plan v3 · Mon Jul 27 – Thu Jul 30

The whole arc in four days

Four weeks of Velocity Arena is really one loop run many times: predict → run → measure the gap → change one thing → predict again. The calendar was never the point; the number of times through the loop is. Gotham's bots drive fine but can't score with untuned stats, so matches keep coming out 0–0 and a 0–0 teaches nothing. So this plan runs the loop on distance instead — measure how far the bot drives — which can never come out 0–0 because it needs no opponent and no goal. It runs that loop about ten times in four days and attaches one piece of evidence to each project goal.

Running the room on Monday? Use the short version. This sheet carries the reasoning, which is for planning. For the table during the day, print “What To Do, Monday to Thursday” — numbered steps, clock times, the exact words to say, and nothing else.
The single principle behind this plan. Do not try to re-teach four weeks of math. Pick one model — a linear equation for how far the bot drives, built from the team's own measurements — and put it through the loop enough times that students can point to a change they made and the improvement it caused. More cycles through one model teach more than one pass through many topics — and cycles are what all four goals actually ask for.

Three moves that make the goals provable

The Mon-collect / Tue-analyze / Wed-assess+prep / Thu-present shape is right and this plan keeps it. But two of the four goals will not happen on their own, so three blocks carry them:

1 · The Target Challenge (the important one). Goal 1 asks students to point to a change that reduced the gap on a later attempt. Matches used to provide those attempts; without them, the Target Challenge does — predict the drive time to stop on a taped line, test it, refine. Introduced Monday afternoon, run hard Tuesday against distances they have never driven. Hitting a distance you never measured is what turns a guess into a model, and it cannot come out 0–0.
2 · Tuesday morning: Model Swap. Goal 2 requires a documented comparison to another team's model and a specific keep/change decision. Students will not do this spontaneously — they will assume the other team is either right or irrelevant. It needs a scheduled half hour, a divergence step so the models actually differ, and a slip of paper.
3 · Wednesday afternoon: teach-back rehearsal. Goal 4's bar is high — a visitor completes the challenge after one explanation. No team clears that bar on the first attempt. They need one rehearsal against a genuinely confused human, and a chance to rewrite the sentence that failed.
Also moved: the Post-Task Diagnostic and Post-Reflection now run first thing Wednesday rather than late in the day. They are the pilot's before/after research measurement and the one thing that cannot be recovered later. Doing them at 10:00 on fresh minds guarantees they happen; anything scheduled after the day's activity is at the mercy of the clock.

Why distance, not matches

The bots drive fine — they just can't score. Scoring on a moving ball needs stats tuned for it, and that is not where this group is, so matches keep coming out 0–0. A 0–0 tells you nothing: no gap to close, no data to model. Running matches "just to try" burns a day for nothing.

Distance solves this exactly. How far a bot drives needs no opponent and no goal — just a tape measure — so it can never come out 0–0. Every bot produces a real number every single run.

Why this is better, not a downgrade

Distance is better raw material than goals would have been. Goals are noisy, small-numbered and depend on the opponent. Distance is a clean linear function of time, measurable to the centimetre, and its parameters are numbers the students control in their own code:

distance = speed × time − startup lag   ⟷   y = m x + b

The slope m is the bot's speed in cm/second — a number they measured themselves, different for every team. The intercept b comes out negative, because the bot loses ground while it accelerates. A student who can say "our intercept is negative because our bot takes a moment to get going" has done exactly what Goal 1 describes.

“When your measurement stops telling you anything, you change the measurement.”

That sentence is the lesson. Choosing what to measure is mathematical modelling — Goal 2, not a consolation prize. Say it plainly; do not apologise for it:

"Matches keep coming out 0–0 because our stats aren't tuned to score. So we predict distance instead — it can't come out 0–0. The number we're predicting changed. The math didn't."
The Target Challenge is the scoring skill, without the 0–0. Stopping the bot on a taped line is precision to a spot — the same aiming a goal needs — but it needs no opponent and no goal, so it always produces a number. It uses the equation backwards: time = (target − start-up) / speed. That is where the predict-then-test loop lives all week.

Tuesday Compare models, then beat the target Tue · Jul 28

The heart of the week. Morning: compare models. Afternoon: prove them against a distance never driven. Goals 1 and 2 both land today.

TimeBlockWhat happens
10:00 – 10:15Hook — read the equations on the wallLook at every team's equation from yesterday's posters. Ask: "Whose bot is fastest? How can you tell just from the equation?" (The speed is the number in front of the time.) Every team's is different — that is correct, do not fix it.
10:15 – 10:50★ MODEL SWAP (Goal 2)First, 10 min of divergence. Everyone followed the same procedure, so without this every equation looks the same and there is nothing to compare. Require each team to make their model visibly different from the reference distance = speed × time − lag in one way:
• add a variable they think matters (battery, surface, start push) • use a different pair of points, giving a different slope • split it in two (a slower first second, then a steady rate) • represent it as a table or graph.
Then swap. Each team studies one other team's model and fills a slip: ① theirs is ____ ② one thing we keep, and why ③ one thing we change, and why — citing a number. Keeping everything is allowed, but defended with a number, not a shrug.

Predict the drive time from the equation (time = (target − start-up) / speed), write it down, then drive and measure the miss. Several rounds at new distances. If a bot will not drive decide by 12:00 Monday

The Distance Lab only needs a bot whose wheels turn, so this is rarer than the match version — but if a bot is dead, do not spend the day troubleshooting it:

  • Triage in order: LEDs showing a number? (if not, re-flash) → batteries + the Cutebot's own power switch → the Cutebot Pro extension in the project.
  • Share working bots. If only one or two drive, teams rotate through them. Each team still writes its own prediction and measures its own trial, so every team keeps its own data and its own equation. Sharing hardware is not sharing an equation.
  • If almost nothing drives, teach Tuesday's method on the practice dataset so the modelling still lands, then have teams rebuild the equation on whatever real numbers they get.

What we are deliberately not doing

Four days cannot hold four weeks. These are cut on purpose, so nobody spends Tuesday morning trying to squeeze them in:

  • Matches, the championship, and awards. The bots can't score, so head-to-head play stays 0–0. The Target Challenge carries the predict-then-test loop instead; the closing is a circle, not a competition.
  • Proportional scaling, decay equations, percent change as standalone lessons. The one model this week is the linear distance equation; that is where the depth goes.
  • Season 2, the Commissioner's Meeting, Rule Proposals, the Underdog Mechanic. Mark Rule Proposals as N/A for Gotham on the submission checklist so it is not chased at the end.
  • Breadth of topics generally. One model, run through the loop about ten times, taught to a stranger. That is the whole plan.
